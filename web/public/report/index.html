Attribution graphs fail because of the derivatives they drop, not because they are linear

Measuring, explaining, predicting and fixing the counterfactual fidelity of transcoder replacement models in GPT-2 small

Technical report, TwinLens project, October 2026. Code, data and figures: https://github.com/SpiliosDimakopoulos/twinlens

Abstract

Attribution graphs explain a language model's computation through an interpretable replacement model: MLPs are swapped for sparse transcoders, while attention patterns and LayerNorm scales are frozen at the clean run. Their edges are read as causal claims: "turn this feature off and the output moves by this much". We test those claims directly. On GPT-2 small with per-layer transcoders, we make 8,064 feature interventions (ablate or amplify ×2) across 56 prompts in 8 task families. We predict each intervention's effect with a ladder of twins, each unfreezing one more component of the real model, and then measure it on the real model.

Four findings:

  1. The linear twin that attribution graphs describe is a weak counterfactual predictor. Its rank correlation with the real effect is ρ = 0.20 [0.14, 0.25]. On edits that measurably move the output, it is within tolerance only 54% [47, 60] of the time. It is worst on attention-mediated tasks: ρ ≈ 0.01 on IOI and 0.07 on induction.
  2. The error comes from two frozen components. Frozen LayerNorm accounts for 88% of the replacement twin's mean error and almost all catastrophic misses. Frozen attention accounts for most of the remaining ranking error.

  3. The fix keeps the graph linear. Freezing a scale or a pattern drops its first-order derivative. Restoring both terms (the LayerNorm-scale Jacobian and the softmax-pattern Jacobian) still gives an exactly linear twin, so effects stay additive over sources. That linear twin matches the fully nonlinear replacement model on every metric:

    • ρ 0.20 → 0.53 [0.49, 0.56] (nonlinear twin: 0.52);
    • sign agreement 67% → 86%;
    • catastrophic misses 2.2% → 0.05% (4 of 8,064 edits);
    • IOI ρ 0.01 → 0.59.

    The fix replicates on a second, architecturally different model, Qwen3-0.6B (§4.7): the paired improvement in ρ is +0.12 [0.05, 0.20] and catastrophic misses fall from 3.0% to 0.8%. The gain is smaller than on GPT-2 but significant on every metric.

    The attribution graph's problem is therefore not linearity but the derivatives it drops. 4. The twin can tell in advance when to trust it. A logistic "trust model" over nine signals computed from the clean run alone predicts whether today's graph prediction will hold. On held-out prompts its AUROC is 0.93. On held-out task families it is 0.93 as well (0.88–0.98). Among the top attribution edges with a noticeable effect, the graph is right 54% of the time overall, 71% on the edges the trust model clears, and 22% on those it flags.

We ship this as a live tool. For any feature you click, TwinLens shows the effect predicted by every twin level, the real effect, and a trust score computed before the real model runs.

1. Motivation

Mechanistic interpretability increasingly explains model behaviour through replacement models: sparse autoencoders, transcoders and cross-layer transcoders, assembled into attribution graphs (Dunefsky et al., 2024; Ameisen et al., 2025; Lindsey et al., 2025). Such a graph is a digital twin of the model's computation on one prompt. Like an industrial digital twin, it is useful only if its simulations agree with the real system under intervention. Ameisen et al. validate graphs with perturbation experiments, and note that the replacement model's agreement with the real model degrades with depth after an intervention. To our knowledge there is no systematic, per-intervention account of when the twin's counterfactual predictions fail, which frozen component causes the failure, and whether failure can be anticipated without running the real model. That third question matters in practice. A user reading a graph cannot re-run every edge as an experiment.

2. Setup

Model. GPT-2 small (124M parameters, 12 layers, 12 heads, d_model = 768). It runs on our own ~250-line PyTorch implementation (twinlens/core/gpt2.py), which exposes TransformerLens-style hook points and reads weights through memory-mapped safetensors. The whole system (model, 12 transcoders, activation caches) fits in 0.82 GB of private memory on a laptop CPU. Logits match Hugging Face GPT2LMHeadModel to 7×10⁻⁵ (max abs).

Twin features. We use the per-layer MLP transcoders for GPT-2 small of Dunefsky, Chlenski & Nanda (2024): 24,576 ReLU features per layer. Each reads LayerNormPre(resid_mid) and predicts mlp_out. We verified this convention empirically: at layer 8 it gives L0 ≈ 66 and FVU 0.41, while the alternatives give FVU > 1. On an 87-token paragraph, splicing a transcoder in place of its MLP recovers 63–94% of the loss gap to mean-ablating that MLP (layers 2, 5, 8, 11).

Validating the instrument. Before making any new measurement, the implementation reproduces published GPT-2 small results. Each one is pinned as a test (tests/test_reproduction.py):

  • Induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022). On repeated random tokens, the five highest induction scores are exactly heads 5.1, 5.5, 6.9, 7.2 and 7.10.
  • Indirect object identification (Wang et al., 2022):
    • The top-3 heads by direct logit attribution to IO − S are the name movers 9.9, 9.6 and 10.0.
    • The two most negative heads are the negative name movers 10.7 and 11.10.
    • Activation patching of heads ranks 9.9 first.
    • Mean-ablating the name movers lowers the logit difference from 2.85 to 2.0, while three random heads leave it unchanged. The drop is only partial, consistent with the backup name movers the paper reports.
  • Causal tracing (Meng et al., 2022):
    • Restoring the residual stream reproduces the early site (last subject token) and the late site (final token).
    • Attention effects concentrate at the final token in layers 8–10.
    • In GPT-2 small the MLP effect at the last subject token peaks in layers 0–1. This is earlier than the mid-layer site reported for GPT-2 XL.
  • Completeness. Per-component direct logit attributions sum to the logit difference (within 10⁻³).

3. The twin ladder

Fix a prompt and its clean run. A feature edit sets transcoder feature f at layer l and position p from its clean activation a to a′: 0 to ablate, 2a to amplify. We compare the change it causes in the log-probability of the clean top-1 token, Δlog p, under six models:

level downstream features LayerNorm scales attention patterns
1 Direct edges held at clean values frozen frozen
2 Linear (graph) ReLU gates frozen (linear) frozen frozen
3 Replacement recomputed (ReLU) frozen frozen
4 + live LayerNorm recomputed live frozen
5 + live attention recomputed live live
Fix A: graph + LN Jacobian ReLU gates frozen (linear) frozen + first-order term frozen
Fix B: graph + LN & attention Jacobians ReLU gates frozen (linear) frozen + first-order term frozen + first-order term
Real GPT-2 real MLPs live live

In every twin level, each MLP is replaced by its transcoder plus that layer's clean-run error term (mlp_out − transcoder(mlp_in)), held fixed. So at zero perturbation every level reproduces the clean logits exactly; this is tested for all six. In the real model, an edit moves the feature's decoder direction, mlp_out[p] += (a′ − a)·W_dec[f], and everything downstream is recomputed.

Level 2 is affine in the perturbation, which is tested. It is exactly the object an attribution graph linearises: graph edges are its partial derivatives. Level 1 keeps only the edited feature's direct path. Its prediction equals the gradient × activation attribution, and our tests check this equality to 1%. Because levels 3–5 each unfreeze one component, the gaps between consecutive levels attribute the twin's error to its sources.

Edits. For each prompt and each layer we take two groups of features:

  • the 3 active features with the largest |direct effect| on the predicted token ("top": the edges a user would inspect first);
  • 3 uniformly random active features ("random").

Each feature is both ablated and amplified. BOS is excluded. The 56 prompts span 8 families: factual recall, IOI, induction/copying, syntax, code, sequences, narrative and sentiment (experiments/prompts.json). That gives 8,064 edits × 6 models.

Metrics.

  • Spearman ρ between predicted and real Δlog p.
  • Median cosine between the predicted and real change in centred logits.
  • Sign agreement, on edits where |real Δlog p| > 0.05.
  • Catastrophic misses: |error| > 1 nat.
  • Within tolerance: |predicted − real| ≤ 0.1 + 0.25·|real|.

Intervals are 95% cluster bootstraps over prompts (1,000 resamples).

Most edits barely matter. The median |real Δlog p| is 0.0024, and only 9.0% of edits move it by more than 0.05. Aggregate tolerance rates are therefore inflated by easy cases. We also report every headline number on this "noticeable effect" subset ("big" below).

The graph twin is a weak counterfactual predictor, and it fails in the cases that matter most. For the 50% of edits taken from the top attribution edges, catastrophic misses rise to 4.2% at level 2, against 0.3% for random features. The edges a user is most likely to act on are the ones most likely to be badly wrong.

Reading a single edge beats propagating through the frozen linear twin. The direct-edge prediction has higher median cosine (0.38 vs 0.27), a higher within-tolerance rate on big edits (73% vs 54%), and almost no catastrophic misses (0.3% vs 2.2%). Propagating a perturbation through frozen LayerNorm amplifies it instead of renormalising it. As a result, adding the indirect paths makes the prediction worse.

4.2 Where the error comes from

Measured by clipped mean error (errors capped at 5 nats), frozen LayerNorm accounts for 88% of the replacement twin's error. Frozen attention accounts for 1.5%, and the transcoders (their reconstruction error, held fixed) for the remaining 11%.

The mechanism is easy to see in single cases. An amplified layer-0 feature whose decoder contribution is 50–90% of the residual-stream norm pushes the frozen-scale twin far outside the transcoders' training distribution. Downstream features then grow layer after layer, and in extreme cases the predicted change in log-probability exceeds 10⁷ nats. The real model, with live LayerNorm, renormalises the perturbation away.

Attention is a small share of mean error but a large share of ranking error. Making it live lifts ρ from 0.31 to 0.52 and sign agreement from 73% to 84%. Its errors are many and moderate, rather than few and catastrophic. The per-family breakdown agrees. At level 2, ρ is 0.01 on IOI and 0.07 on induction (both attention-mediated circuits) but 0.36 on factual recall. At level 5 all families sit between 0.43 and 0.58.

4.3 Fidelity decays with depth

The median cosine between predicted and real Δlogits falls with the number of layers between the edit and the output, at every level. The decline matches the per-layer compounding Ameisen et al. describe. For the graph twin it goes from 0.71 (0–2 layers) to 0.05 (9–11 layers). For the live-attention twin it goes from 0.89 to 0.32.

4.4 Predicting when the twin is wrong

The trust model predicts within tolerance for the graph twin from nine signals of the clean run, with no access to the real model's response. Under 5-fold cross-validation grouped by prompt:

  • AUROC 0.93 over all edits (base rate 90%).
  • AUROC 0.84 on big edits, where the base rate is only 54%.
  • The best single signal, relative edit size (‖Δa·W_dec‖ / ‖resid‖), reaches 0.90. Activation reaches 0.85, layers to output 0.78, and |direct effect| 0.78.
  • Trusted vs flagged. Ranking edits by predicted trust, the top half is within tolerance 99.8% of the time and the bottom half 80.6%.
  • Calibration. Calibration is reasonable at the top, where most edits sit: predicted 0.95 vs observed 0.96. It is slightly over-confident between 0.3 and 0.7.

The standardised coefficients are interpretable. Trust falls with edit size (−0.53), activation (−0.66), the number of layers to the output (−0.53), the number of active downstream features (−0.33), and attention from the output position to the edited token (−0.30). It rises with model confidence (+0.56).

4.5 The fix: keep the derivatives, keep the linearity

Freezing a LayerNorm scale σ at its clean value is a zeroth-order approximation of σ. A first-order linearisation of the normalised output n = u/σ(u) around the clean point gives

dn = da − n₀ (n₀ · da) / d,      where a = u / σ₀ is the frozen-scale output.

Freezing σ keeps only da. Similarly, freezing an attention pattern drops

dp = p₀ ⊙ (ds − Σₖ p₀ ds),       with ds = (dq · k₀ + q₀ · dk) / √d_head,

together with the bilinear term in z = p·v, which we remove so that z = p₀v + dp·v₀.

Restoring these terms costs nothing in linearity:

  • Fix B is affine in the perturbation, which is tested.
  • It is the exact first-order expansion of the gated-feature twin with live LayerNorm and attention. On a 1% edit it agrees with that twin to < 5%, while the frozen versions do not (tests/test_twin.py).
  • In practice, it is the attribution graph you get by not detaching the LayerNorm scales and attention scores when backpropagating.

  • Fix A alone captures the entire benefit of live LayerNorm. It matches level 4 on every metric while staying linear. It cuts mean error by 79%, and catastrophic misses among the top edges from 4.2% to 0.47%.
  • Fix B matches or exceeds the nonlinear twin on every metric. Top-edge catastrophic misses fall to 0.10%. Since Fix B is linear and the nonlinear twin is not, the remaining gap to the real model is due to the transcoders, not to linearisation.

The gains are largest exactly where today's graphs fail: IOI ρ goes from 0.01 to 0.59 and induction from 0.07 to 0.48. These are the attention-mediated circuits, where freezing attention patterns removes the mechanism itself.

4.6 Does the trust model transfer, and what does it buy?

  • Transfer. Leaving out one task family at a time (training on 7, testing on the 8th), the trust model's AUROC is 0.93 on average, ranging from 0.88 (sentiment) to 0.98 (sequences). It is not memorising task types.

  • Payoff. Consider the edges a user would actually inspect: the top attribution edges, on edits with a noticeable effect (n = 718).

    • Today's graph is within tolerance on 54% of them.
    • Keeping only the edges the trust model clears (66% of them) raises that to 71%.
    • The flagged third is right only 22% of the time.

    A one-line filter therefore turns a coin flip into a usable signal, without running a single extra intervention.

4.7 Does the fix generalise? Qwen3-0.6B

We replicated Fix B (graph + LayerNorm and attention Jacobians, level linear_fulljac) on a second, architecturally different model: Qwen3-0.6B, which uses RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, grouped-query attention, RoPE, QK-norm and SwiGLU. We used its public circuit-tracer transcoders (163,840 features per layer, mwhanna/qwen3-0.6b-transcoders-lowl0). The scope is smaller than for GPT-2: 2,304 feature edits on 16 prompts (2 per task family), with edits in layers 10-27 of 28 (the upper two thirds). The levels compared are direct edges, graph today (linear), Fix A (adding the normalisation Jacobian, here RMSNorm including QK-norm), Fix B, and the real model. The nonlinear replacement twins were not run on Qwen3, because a full 164k-feature encode per simulation is too slow on CPU.

The Jacobians are verified by tests on Qwen3 as well (tests/test_qwen.py): every level reproduces the clean run, the linear levels are exactly affine, and Fix B agrees with the live-norm/live-attention twin to within 5% on a 1% edit.

Fix A 0.42 [0.32, 0.50] 85.6% 58.6% 1.4% 0.083

On induction prompts ρ rises from 0.04 to 0.28.

Paired improvement of Fix B over graph today (same edits, 95% cluster bootstrap over prompts):

  • GPT-2 small: Δρ +0.33 [0.27, 0.39]; Δ sign agreement +19 pp [15, 24]; Δ within tolerance (big) +26 pp [20, 32]; Δ catastrophic -2.2 pp [-2.7, -1.7].
  • Qwen3-0.6B: Δρ +0.12 [0.05, 0.20]; Δ sign agreement +9 pp [2, 17]; Δ within tolerance (big) +10 pp [3, 18]; Δ catastrophic -2.2 pp [-3.7, -0.8].

All intervals exclude zero. The direction of the gain is the same on every metric, but its magnitude is smaller on Qwen3. One candidate reason is the larger residual transcoder error of this transcoder family, which no Jacobian can repair; we have not tested this. The gain is also not uniform across task families: it is largest on induction, syntax, sentiment and IOI, and roughly flat on code and narrative.

5. Limitations

  • Two models, two transcoder families. GPT-2 small with per-layer transcoders, and Qwen3-0.6B with circuit-tracer transcoders, where we covered only the upper 18 layers and far fewer edits (§4.7). Larger models and cross-layer transcoders, which the current circuit-tracing work uses, remain untested.
  • The intervention is defined in the twin's basis. In the real model we move the feature's decoder direction rather than "the concept". This is the natural ground truth for the claim "this edge matters", but it is not the only possible one.
  • The tolerance threshold is a choice (0.1 nats + 25%). The ladder's ordering is the same under rank correlation and cosine, which use no threshold.
  • Small prompt set (56 prompts, written by us), and only one prediction target per prompt (the top-1 token). The bootstrap is over prompts, so the intervals reflect this.
  • Graphs built on Fix B are denser. Edges then also flow through attention scores (QK paths), not only OV paths. Effects remain additive and exact for the linear twin, but we have not measured how much this increases graph size after pruning.
  • The trust model is fitted to this distribution of edits (top and random features, ablate and ×2). Its transfer to other edit types, such as steering with large coefficients, is untested.

6. Related work

  • Circuits and their validation. Elhage et al. (2021) give the framework. Olsson et al. (2022) and Wang et al. (2022) describe the circuits we reproduce. Meng et al. (2022) introduced causal tracing. On activation-patching practice: Zhang & Nanda (2024); Heimersheim & Nanda (2024).
  • Sparse replacement models.
    • Sparse autoencoders: Bricken et al. (2023); Cunningham et al. (2023).
    • Sparse feature circuits: Marks et al. (2024).
    • Transcoders: Dunefsky, Chlenski & Nanda (2024).
    • Attribution graphs with cross-layer transcoders (Ameisen et al., 2025; Lindsey et al., 2025), and the open-source circuit-tracer library.
  • Faithfulness of replacement models. Ameisen et al. report that perturbation agreement decays with depth. Our contributions:
    • decomposing that error into frozen-LN, frozen-attention and transcoder components, on a large sampled set of interventions;
    • showing that restoring the two dropped first-order terms removes it without giving up linearity;
    • showing that the remaining failures can be anticipated from the clean run.
